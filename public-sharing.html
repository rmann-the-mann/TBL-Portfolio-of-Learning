Public Sharing and Collaborative Development Toward the end of the portfolio-development process, once I had a complete draft in place, I actively sought feedback from my TBL module team, The Terriﬁc Twos. Patricia Quigley graciously offered her feedback. The conversations provided both positive and constructive feedback. Patricia identiﬁed strengths within the portfolio, including the documentation of learning, use of technology, and the scholarly comparison of TBL with other active learning approaches. More importantly, the discussion prompted me to revisit several sections of the portfolio to ensure that learning outcomes were not only identiﬁed but explicitly connected to the accompanying artifacts and evidence of learning. The conversation also highlighted opportunities to strengthen the use of TBL-speciﬁc terminology within my lesson plan. Through sharing work publicly with a peer, inviting critique, and incorporating feedback into subsequent revisions, I was able to reﬁne both the portfolio itself and my understanding of the assessment expectations. To make the exchange between Patricia and me accessible within the portfolio, I used Microsoft Copilot to organize the transcribed audio messages and typed WhatsApp messages into a coherent conversation. The reconstruction is based on the accompanying screenshots and transcripts. Screenshots are included below as the original evidence. Minor edits were made for readability without changing the substance of the exchange. Note: I was unable to ﬁnd a public sharing area on the Moodle. So while I was unable to share with the entirety of the class, I was able to share and collaborate with my team. Portfolio Feedback Conversation with Patricia Quigley Patricia reviewed the complete portfolio and identiﬁed three areas for attention: making the links between artifacts and learning outcomes explicit, using TBL terminology more consistently in the lesson plan, and checking alignment against the sometimes inconsistent assessment documents. Our discussion also tested my interpretation of the scholarly-introduction requirement. I retained the broader comparison of TBL with other active-learning approaches because it demonstrates critical framing, while revising the Documentation of Learning and lesson terminology in response to her feedback. Selected original messages are retained below as evidence of the exchange. Ryan:

Revisions Following the Conversation Following our conversation, I revised the Documentation of Learning to explain more clearly how the artifacts demonstrate achievement of the module learning outcomes. I also reviewed the lesson plan to ensure that some of the terminology was consistent with the TBL process but I also wanted to stay consistent with lesson planning at SAIT. Feedback for Patricia I enjoyed providing feedback to Patricia. I started by using my iPad to annotate my notes in her PDF, then I voice recorded my thoughts and used the transcript as the notes. I then used ChatGPT to summarize the notes in a way that was clear and understandable. Screenshots of parts of the feedback interaction are below. For her scholarly introduction, I highlighted its clarity and thoroughness, the balanced discussion of TBL’s strengths and limitations, and particularly strong sections on peer accountability, team community, graduate attributes, and practical strategies for managing different rates of student progress. My main suggestions for her documentation of learning were to strengthen a few claims with evidence or comparison, clarify some wording, and add nuance where the literature may present different perspectives. In particular, I encouraged Patricia to revisit claims about team formation, student buy-in, classroom conﬁguration, preparation materials, and the effectiveness of TBL relative to other approaches.

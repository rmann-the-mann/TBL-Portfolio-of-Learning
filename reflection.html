Reﬂection and Future Application One of the things I appreciate most about Team-Based Learning (TBL) is how thoughtful and intentional the process is. Each stage has a clear purpose, from preparing students before class to individual accountability, team discussion, application, and feedback (Michaelsen & Sweet, 2008). While I do not see myself adopting the full TBL model, several elements could strengthen my approach to active and collaborative learning. I particularly value the accountability built into the iRAT and tRAT process. Students must ﬁrst demonstrate that they have engaged with the pre-class learning before revisiting the same questions as a team. This iterative process makes individual thinking visible before students learn through discussion with their peers (Michaelsen & Sweet, 2008; Parmelee et al., 2012). The immediate feedback during the tRAT is brilliant because students can address misconceptions while they are still engaged with the content. The resulting data also provides valuable formative assessment. Rather than reteaching everything, I can focus on speciﬁc muddy points without turning the class back into a full-blown lecture. Many of the features within LAMS support this process well, including the scratch cards, student-response data, and ability to share burning questions. That said, I found LAMS quite clunky as an in-class tool. Waiting for teams to organize, select leaders, open gates, and reach the same stage consumed a fair amount of class time. Proﬁciency would likely improve this, but I would want the technology to become much more invisible within the learning experience. If managing the system adds too much cognitive load or interrupts the ﬂow of discussion, it begins to work against the active learning it is intended to support. My greatest hesitation with TBL is its uniformity. Its structure is part of why it works, but I also think it can quench creativity. The 4S framework, signiﬁcant problem, same problem, speciﬁc choice, and simultaneous reveal, is the structure which application exercises should follow (Michaelsen & Sweet, 2008). Within the 4S framework, I strongly agree with Signiﬁcant Problem and Same Problem. Meaningful and authentic problems are important for student engagement, while having teams address the same problem creates a shared foundation for discussion. Simultaneous Reporting also requires teams to commit to their thinking before hearing from others, making differences in reasoning visible and creating richer opportunities for discussion. Speciﬁc Choice is where I struggle. I can see its value when students are evaluating deﬁned alternatives because the constraint forces teams to compare options, make a decision, and defend their reasoning.

However, it may also place boundaries around higher-level creative thinking. Instead of asking students to determine what a solution could be, we ask them to choose which of our solutions is best. The issue is therefore one of alignment. Speciﬁc Choice seems well suited to learning outcomes focused on evaluation and decision-making, but less appropriate when I want students to generate possibilities I may not have anticipated. The module also reinforced TBL’s connection to ﬂipped learning. Flipped approaches depend on students engaging with the learning before class, and TBL addresses this through built-in accountability (Hew & Lo, 2018; Michaelsen & Sweet, 2008). However, pre-class learning must also be purposeful, manageable, and clearly connected to what students will do with it. Accountability cannot compensate for poorly designed preparation. The readiness assurance process works because the preparation, assessment, and application activities are constructively aligned (Parmelee et al., 2012). Ultimately, I am unlikely to use TBL exactly as designed, but I do not think I need to. I can incorporate several of its principles into my practice, particularly intentional team formation, stronger accountability for pre-class learning, and three elements of the 4S framework. In a future workshop, I plan to trial a short individual quiz followed by team re-evaluation. I can compare the individual and team responses to identify misconceptions and determine what requires further discussion. I would also gather brief student feedback to evaluate whether the process improved preparation and the quality of team discussion. For me, the value of TBL is not in adopting the entire framework, but in using its strongest elements to make active and collaborative learning more intentional and effective.

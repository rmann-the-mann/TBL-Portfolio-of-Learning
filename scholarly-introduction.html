Team-Based Learning: Scholarly Introduction Team-Based Learning (TBL) is a structured active-learning methodology designed to promote student preparation, collaboration, and the application of knowledge. Originally developed by Larry Michaelsen for use in large university classes, TBL sought to retain the beneﬁts of small-group learning while working within the realities of larger class sizes (Michaelsen et al., 2002; Parmelee et al., 2012). Rather than positioning the instructor as the person responsible for delivering content, TBL shifts greater responsibility for initial knowledge acquisition to students and creates more classroom time for collaborative learning and application. Importantly, TBL is more than simply adding group activities to a traditional classroom. Michaelsen and Sweet (2008) describe TBL as an integrated instructional strategy in which each component supports the others. It is this deliberate structure, rather than simply the presence of teams, that distinguishes TBL from more informal approaches to collaborative learning. The TBL learning cycle begins with assigned pre-class student preparation, followed by a readiness assurance process and team application exercises (Michaelsen & Sweet, 2008; Parmelee et al., 2012). Students ﬁrst complete an Individual Readiness Assurance Test (iRAT) before completing the same assessment collaboratively through a Team Readiness Assurance Test (tRAT). This sequence creates both individual and team accountability while providing immediate feedback and opportunities for peer-to-peer learning. Following a brief clariﬁcation of challenging concepts from the instructor, students move into application exercises structured around four principles referred to as the 4S Framework: a signiﬁcant problem, the same problem for all teams, a speciﬁc choice, and simultaneous reporting (Michaelsen & Sweet, 2008). Together, these elements create a learning environment where students are expected to come prepared, contribute to their team, make decisions, defend their thinking, and apply their knowledge to meaningful problems. The strength of TBL, therefore, is not found in any one of these activities, but in how preparation, accountability, feedback, discussion, and application are intentionally connected. TBL shares characteristics with other active, learner-focused approaches, including ﬂipped learning, problem-based learning (PBL), and project-based learning (PjBL), but differs in the degree and purpose of its instructional structure. Like ﬂipped learning, TBL moves much of the initial engagement with course content outside of class so that class time can focus on active application. Research suggests that ﬂipped learning can improve student achievement, particularly when pre-class preparation is reinforced through structured in-class activities such as quizzes (Hew & Lo, 2018). TBL takes this further by making readiness

assurance, stable teams, immediate feedback, and structured application central to the learning process. PBL, in comparison, places greater emphasis on student-directed inquiry into complex and often open- ended problems, while TBL provides common preparation and a more standardized learning experience (Parmelee et al., 2012). Recent comparative evidence suggests that TBL may produce stronger theoretical knowledge and teamwork outcomes than PBL, although these advantages do not consistently extend to practical skills, learning interest, or understanding (Zhang et al., 2024). PjBL also emphasizes collaborative and authentic learning but generally involves extended inquiry leading to the development of a product or solution (Kokotsaki et al., 2016). Rather than viewing these approaches as competing methodologies, the literature suggests their value is better considered in relation to the learning outcomes they are intended to support. From this perspective, TBL offers a particularly structured approach for moving students from foundational knowledge toward collaborative application. Research generally supports TBL as an effective approach to post-secondary learning, although the strength of that evidence depends on the outcome being examined. Fatmi et al. (2013) found that TBL may improve knowledge scores, but learner reactions were mixed and the evidence base was limited by methodological weaknesses. Swanson et al. (2019) reported a moderate positive mean effect on content knowledge (g = 0.55), but none of the included studies met all ﬁve design indicators used in the review. The results suggest that TBL is effective when its elements work together, although it remains unclear which elements contribute most to the effect. These ﬁndings provide support for the combination of preparation, individual accountability, peer discussion, and immediate feedback that underpins the TBL model. TBL may therefore be particularly useful when students need to develop a common body of foundational knowledge before applying that knowledge collaboratively. However, positive ﬁndings should not be interpreted as evidence that TBL is universally more effective than other active-learning approaches. Much of the empirical research on TBL has also been conducted within medicine and health-professions education (Fatmi et al., 2013; Swanson et al., 2019), which raises questions about how readily these ﬁndings can be generalized across other post-secondary disciplines and learning environments. Direct comparisons with other active-learning approaches further complicate claims that TBL is inherently superior. Comparisons between TBL and ﬂipped learning, for example, have identiﬁed differences in student preparation, attitudes, and perceptions of teaching without necessarily ﬁnding signiﬁcant differences in ﬁnal examination performance (Hayama et al., 2021). Similarly, comparisons between TBL and PBL suggest that the relative effectiveness of each approach depends partly on what is being measured, with TBL showing advantages in some knowledge and teamwork outcomes but not across all

areas of learning (Zhang et al., 2024). This distinction is important when considering TBL from an educator's perspective. The question is not simply whether TBL produces better results than another methodology, but whether its structures support the knowledge, skills, and behaviours students are expected to develop. The available evidence supports TBL primarily as a deliberately structured approach to active learning rather than as a replacement for other student-centred methodologies. Overall, the literature positions TBL as a structured approach to active and collaborative learning in which preparation, accountability, immediate feedback, and application are intentionally connected (Michaelsen & Sweet, 2008). Research provides meaningful support for its effectiveness, particularly in relation to content knowledge and teamwork, while also demonstrating that outcomes depend on context, implementation, and what is being measured (Swanson et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2024). This suggests that the value of TBL lies less in whether it is superior to other active-learning approaches and more in the intentionality of its design and its alignment with intended learning outcomes. This portfolio documents my participation and development as a TBL facilitator through the design, application, facilitation, and learning activities completed during the TBL Certiﬁcate module.
